## Problem, approach, and differentiation

**Robotics intelligence is advancing from specialized behaviors toward generalist models.** Vision-language-action models (VLAs) connect visual observations and language instructions to robot actions, allowing knowledge to be shared across tasks and embodiments. Physical Intelligence’s π₀.₅ demonstrates generalization through training on heterogeneous sources, including robot experience, semantic prediction, and web data. Its subsequent π*₀.₆ work incorporates demonstrations, human corrections, and autonomous experience to improve reliability and throughput. These results establish generalist learning and reinforcement learning as credible routes toward useful robotic manipulation. [π₀.₅ research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16054); [π*₀.₆ research](https://www.pi.website/blog/pistar06).

The field is also developing architectures that separate high-level reasoning from physical execution. Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics work combines embodied reasoning with action generation; its current embodied-reasoning model delegates motor execution to lower-level VLAs. Earlier research such as SayCan grounds language-based skill selection in learned estimates of whether a robot can successfully execute each skill. These approaches demonstrate that general-purpose robotics can involve multiple interacting models, with different components responsible for understanding instructions, selecting behaviors, and controlling motion. [Gemini Robotics](https://deepmind.google/models/gemini-robotics/embodied-reasoning/); [SayCan](https://say-can.github.io/).

**Our target is the incremental cost of expanding a robot’s useful capabilities.** These advances make the competitive question more specific: how much additional data, training, engineering, and validation are required to enable the next workflow at a commercially useful level of reliability? A robot may be able to grasp a bowl, place objects on a tray, and avoid obstacles, yet still need adaptation to combine those capabilities in a crowded restaurant workspace. Episodic’s investment thesis is that explicitly reusable physical knowledge can reduce that adaptation burden, improving the economics of deploying existing robots across additional workflows.

### Research foundation: reusable models, composition, and physical feasibility

Episodic’s proposed approach draws on a research tradition that combines learning with structured planning and control. Three connected foundations are particularly relevant.

**Learning models of reusable skills.** Research by Zi Wang, Caelan Garrett, Leslie Pack Kaelbling, and Tomás Lozano-Pérez studies how robots can learn models of when sensorimotor skills will succeed, then use those models to plan novel combinations of skills. Their system learns constraints on skill effectiveness from limited examples and combines those learned models with task and motion planning. The relevant principle is that reusable behavior requires knowledge of the conditions under which a skill works and the effects it can achieve. _[Learning compositional models of robot skills for task and motion planning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06444)_.

Related work by Garrett, Lozano-Pérez, and Kaelbling, PDDLStream, connects symbolic task planning with procedures that generate continuous quantities such as grasps, placements, and trajectories. This addresses a central robotics challenge: a sequence of sensible task steps must also admit physically feasible motions. For Episodic, this work supports treating workflow generation as a problem involving both task structure and continuous physical constraints. _[PDDLStream](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08705)_.

**Composing learned constraints to solve unfamiliar combinations.** Yilun Du’s dissertation, _Learning Generalizable Systems by Learning Composable Energy Landscapes_, develops a framework in which models assign low energy to plausible or desirable solutions. At inference time, the system searches for solutions under a combination of learned models and task requirements. A model of feasible trajectories can therefore be reused with different goals or constraints, rather than retrained for each objective. In the dissertation’s block-stacking experiments, one trained model supports several task specifications by changing the constraint functions used during planning. [Du, 2024, supplied dissertation, §6.1.]

A direct bridge between Du’s and Kaelbling’s research is _Compositional Diffusion-Based Continuous Constraint Solvers_, coauthored with Zhutian Yang, Jiayuan Mao, Jiajun Wu, Joshua Tenenbaum, and Tomás Lozano-Pérez. Diffusion-CCSP represents a problem as a graph of interacting variables and constraints, combining learned diffusion models for individual constraint types to search for a jointly compatible solution. The research demonstrates generalization to new combinations of known constraints and integration with task and motion planning. This provides a concrete technical precedent for Episodic’s hypothesis that physical knowledge can be learned in reusable components and recombined for new tasks. _[Compositional Diffusion-Based Continuous Constraint Solvers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00966)_.

**Composing behaviors while accounting for uncertainty.** Anirudha Majumdar and Russ Tedrake’s work on funnel libraries provides a complementary foundation from feedback control. Their method precomputes motion primitives together with regions describing how the system can evolve under bounded disturbances, then composes compatible primitives at runtime. Its robustness guarantees depend on the specified models and assumptions. The relevant lesson for Episodic is that reliable composition requires understanding when components can connect and how execution uncertainty affects the resulting behavior. These guarantees do not automatically extend to learned energy-based models. _[Funnel Libraries for Real-Time Robust Feedback Motion Planning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.04037)_.

Tedrake’s work with Tobia Marcucci and colleagues on motion planning through Graphs of Convex Sets offers another complementary approach: structuring collision-constrained trajectory planning so that powerful optimization methods can solve it efficiently. Together, these research directions motivate combining learned representations with explicit planning and physical feasibility checks where appropriate. _[Motion Planning around Obstacles with Convex Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04422)_.

### Our approach

Episodic intends to build compositional robotics models that make reusable physical knowledge the basis of workflow expansion. Given an observed environment and a task, the system would combine relevant learned components with task requirements and search for a compatible plan. Those components could represent aspects of feasible motion, object interactions, placement relationships, or skill effectiveness. When an existing library is insufficient, our goal is to learn the missing capability and incorporate it for subsequent reuse.

For example, clearing a table and loading a service tray share requirements involving grasping, reachability, placement, and collision avoidance. The workflows differ in their goals and arrangement constraints. Our hypothesis is that a system with reusable representations of those shared requirements can adapt more efficiently than one that requires substantial additional training or engineering for each complete workflow. This remains a hypothesis to validate on physical systems.

### Alternatives and proposed differentiation

Several existing approaches address parts of this problem. Generalist VLAs pursue broad transfer through shared representations and diverse training data. Reinforcement learning improves behavior through experience and feedback. Diffusion Policy learns distributions over action sequences from demonstrations. Language-guided systems such as SayCan compose available skills, while task and motion planners combine discrete decisions with continuous feasibility reasoning. These are credible alternatives and potential components of a practical robotics system. [π₀.₅](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16054); [π*₀.₆](https://www.pi.website/blog/pistar06); [Diffusion Policy](https://diffusion-policy.cs.columbia.edu/diffusion_policy_2023.pdf); [SayCan](https://say-can.github.io/); [PDDLStream](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08705).

Episodic’s proposed differentiation is the explicit composition of learned physical models and constraints at inference time, with success measured by the cost of adding useful capabilities. This could complement pretrained perception, language, and action models. Using diffusion or composing skills is already established research; our advantage must come from the implementation and its demonstrated effect on adaptation economics.

We will compare against relevant learned policies and planning systems using consistent hardware, task definitions, and success criteria. The central measures are additional demonstrations, training and integration effort, task success, planning latency, intervention frequency, and performance retained on earlier workflows. Evaluations must distinguish unfamiliar combinations of known capabilities from tasks that require genuinely new physical knowledge.

The research provides a credible foundation while identifying important execution challenges. Du’s dissertation highlights difficult optimization landscapes and uncertainty about how to discover the correct compositional structure. Episodic must demonstrate that the benefits of reuse exceed the costs of search, component development, integration, and validation. The commercial opportunity is to translate these foundations into a system that reliably lowers the incremental cost of enabling additional robot workflows.
